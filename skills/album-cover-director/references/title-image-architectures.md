# Title-image architectures

Use this reference before writing a cover prompt when the release title should
be the image's organizing force. An architecture is a relationship between the
exact title and the image, not a font style or a genre preset. Choose one per
direction. Do not reproduce a reference cover's lettering, palette, motifs, or
layout.

Every reviewed candidate must contain the exact title once. The title is the
largest or most structurally decisive visual form, while song-derived details
are discovered after the title is read.

## 1. Title map

**Use when:** the song has routes, systems, memories, crowds, fragments, or a
world with many connected motifs.

**Geometry:** a large continuous title skeleton occupies roughly half the
square. Its counters, loops, and strokes become a map of spaces; motifs grow
from, pass through, or inhabit those spaces.

**Prompt requirements:** exact title; title share of square; one continuous
line or constructed skeleton; 6–12 lyric motifs with locations inside, on, or
outside named letter areas; title silhouette as the 56px signal.

**Reject when:** motifs are stickers around ordinary display type, or detail
overwhelms the title silhouette.

## 2. Enclosing contour

**Use when:** one object, aperture, person, or event is the song's emotional
center.

**Geometry:** title lettering forms one asymmetric contour around a central
motif. The shared baseline bends with that motif's gravity, as if the title is
a ribbon, cable, liquid trace, or other song-derived carrier.

**Prompt requirements:** identify the central motif; define the title's
continuous carrier and reading route; specify where the contour touches,
crosses, or disappears behind the central motif.

**Reject when:** the title becomes a circular stamp, a neat border, or a
caption placed around an unrelated object.

## 3. Kinetic wordmark and evidence field

**Use when:** rhythm, velocity, release, pressure, or a repeated signal is the
track's central force.

**Geometry:** a large interlocking wordmark fills the upper or central two
thirds. A denser lower or secondary field repeats non-linguistic marks made by
the same stroke system: routes, waveforms, scratches, fragments, or material
traces.

**Prompt requirements:** title as one interlocking stroke system; wordmark
share of square; distinct primary and secondary density zones; all supporting
marks explicitly non-linguistic; a palette relationship rather than a named
reference palette.

**Reject when:** the lower field introduces pseudo-text, tags, or a competing
second title.

## 4. Palimpsest intervention

**Use when:** the song concerns erasure, civic pressure, memory, conflict,
re-recording, or a place being written over.

**Geometry:** one central scene or object exists on a surface repeatedly
crossed by the title. The title can be in front of and behind selected forms,
but its reading path stays intact. Supporting marks are erased routes,
non-linguistic notation, material scratches, or paint evidence.

**Prompt requirements:** name the central scene; specify title depth order;
identify one process (painted, scraped, reprinted, taped, or overwritten);
state which image elements may occlude the title and which may not.

**Reject when:** the title is a generic graffiti tag, every mark is equally
legible, or the scene becomes a backdrop for a caption.

## 5. Emblem orbit

**Use when:** the track has one iconic object, identity, ritual, portal, or
record-like centre that benefits from an immediate poster read.

**Geometry:** one central medallion controls the square. The title is split
into two or more large arcs that follow the same radial geometry. Type and
central image are separate materials but share one centre of gravity.

**Prompt requirements:** central medallion; exact title split and reading
order; arc positions; radial relation; title weight larger than any supporting
detail; a field color that lets the type silhouette dominate.

**Reject when:** type sits mechanically above and below a framed image, or the
medallion is so detailed that the title loses first read.

## From reference to prompt

When a user supplies a cover reference or a web discovery is considered:

1. Name the architecture and only the observable relationship it demonstrates.
2. Replace its specific title, subjects, palette, lettering, and layout with
   track-derived choices.
3. Write the prompt from the selected architecture's requirements.
4. Record the source link and the derived relationship, but never store the
   reference raster in the repository.
5. Add a new architecture only when it cannot be expressed by an existing
   card and it yields a distinct, successful title-image relationship in a
   real cover run.
